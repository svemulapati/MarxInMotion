# Tiny Town continuity

Fixed facts every chapter must respect, so the story builds instead of resetting.

## Cast (lib/cast.ts)

Leo the weaver, Ava the tailor, Mia the baker, Sam the worker, Mr. Moneybags the boss,
Grandpa Ned the farmer, Bea the banker, Tom the shopkeeper. One-off extras are drawn gray
(`<Figure name="..." />`) and must not join the recurring cast.

## The story so far

- Ch 1 to 3: Leo, Ava and Mia make and swap things; gold coins become money.
- Ch 4: Mr. Moneybags arrives with 100 coins and buys to sell for more. Old Silas is a miser (one-off).
- Ch 5: Moneybags cannot get richer just by buying and selling fairly.
- From Ch 6: Sam owns nothing but his ability to work and sells it to Mr. Moneybags by the day.
  Moneybags opens **a spinning workshop** in Tiny Town (Marx's own example: cotton spun into yarn).
- Leo stays an independent hand-loom weaver. (Ch 15: machine looms ruin hand-loom weavers, as in Marx.)
- Mia's bakery is where long hours and night work show up (Ch 10: Marx's journeyman bakers).

## Story so far, Ch 5 to 18 (already written)

- Ch 5-6: cheating only moves coins around; Sam sells his labour-power by the day for 3 coins.
- Ch 7-9: the spinning workshop; 27 coins in, 30 out; Senior's "last hour" (Prof. Senior, one-off).
- Ch 10: the fight over the clock (1349 to 1866); Mia once was a baker's helper in the big city.
- Ch 11: one worker or a hundred; Leo as a self-employed weaver vs a "small master".
- Ch 12: cheaper bread (Mia's new oven) shrinks Sam's paid part to 4 hours (no law fixes the day).
- Ch 13-14: twelve masons; Moneybags' carriage workshop where Ava is reduced to sewing cushions.
- Ch 15: machines; Sam's mum, sister and brother join the mill; Leo ruined by the power loom; Luddites.
- Ch 16-18: Miss Rose and Miss Kim (teachers, one-offs); paid and unpaid hours; rent goes to a gray
  "Landlord" one-off, never to Grandpa Ned.

## Plan for Ch 19 to 33 (binding)

- **Wages (19-22):** Sam is paid by the hour, then by the piece; the wage *looks* like payment for the
  whole day. Ch 22 compares Tiny Town with a faraway country.
- **Accumulation (23-25):** Moneybags at first spends the surplus on himself (23), then reinvests it so
  the workshop grows each year (24; "Accumulate, accumulate!"). In 25 bigger machines mean fewer
  workers per coin; a crowd of out-of-work people waits at the workshop gate (the industrial reserve
  army). Sam's own job is never safe.
- **Primitive accumulation (26-33):** the backstory. Long ago Sam's great-grandparents farmed common
  land outside Tiny Town; it was fenced off (enclosure) and they were driven into town with nothing
  but their hands. Grandpa Ned is the descendant of small farmers who became a *tenant* farmer paying
  rent (Ch 29 can show how farmers like him rose); he is never the landlord. A gray "Lord of the
  Manor" one-off does the fencing. Ch 33: Mr. Moneybags tries to start a workshop in a far colony
  with free land (Marx's Mr. Peel and the Swan River).
- **Ch 32** states Marx's prediction ("the expropriators are expropriated") as his argument, with
  critics given fair space. No cheerleading, no mockery.

## Volume II plan (binding)

Volume I watched value being *made*. Volume II follows capital as it *moves*.

- **The circuit (Ch 1-6):** Mr. Moneybags' coins (M) buy cotton, spindles and Sam's labour-power (C);
  the workshop runs (P); out comes yarn worth more (C′), which must be sold to become more coins (M′).
  Write it M–C…P…C′–M′. Selling the yarn is never instant: carts, shops, and waiting.
  One-offs for costs of circulation: a clerk ("Clerk Pim") doing the books, a carter hauling yarn.
  Transport counts as production in Marx; bookkeeping and selling time do not add value.
- **Turnover (Ch 7-17):** the cotton is used up and replaced every week (circulating capital);
  the spinning machine lasts for years and passes its value on slowly (fixed capital).
  Grandpa Ned's wheat takes months to grow (production time longer than working time).
  Yarn shipped to a far country takes weeks to sell (circulation time). Use Marx's own numbers
  where the chapter gives them (e.g. a 9-week working period with 3 weeks' circulation in Ch 15,
  capitals A and B in Ch 16) and label any conversion to coins.
- **Reproduction (Ch 18-21):** the whole town split into two departments.
  **Department I makes means of production:** the town Forge (machines, tools; gray one-off
  "the Ironmaster") and Moneybags' spinning mill. **Department II makes things people consume:**
  Mia's bakery and Ava's tailoring. Use Marx's own schemes and numbers (e.g. simple reproduction
  I: 4000c + 1000v + 1000s, II: 2000c + 500v + 500s, so I(v+s) = IIc = 2000), scaled only if labelled.
- Vol II was assembled by **Engels** from Marx's manuscripts; chapters may say so where the text is
  unfinished (Engels' notes in the text).

## Volume III plan (binding)

Volume I showed surplus-value being made, Volume II showed it travelling. Volume III shows how it is
*shared out* and why the sharing hides where it came from. The surplus is a pie; the town fights
over slices.

- **Profit (Ch 1-7):** Mr. Moneybags stops thinking in c, v and s. He sees only cost-price (c + v)
  and profit; the rate of profit s/(c+v) is always smaller than s/v. Use the workshop numbers
  (c 24, v 3, s 3: rate of surplus-value 100%, rate of profit 3/27 = 11.1%) when the chapter
  doesn't give its own.
- **Average profit (Ch 8-12):** many workshops in Tiny Town with different mixes of machines and
  workers (e.g. Moneybags' machine-heavy mill vs Ava's labour-heavy tailoring). Competition moves
  capital until every branch earns the same average rate; prices of production replace values.
  Use Marx's own five-capital table in Ch 9 exactly. The "transformation problem" critics belong here.
- **Falling rate of profit (Ch 13-15):** more machines per worker; the rate falls even as the mass
  of profit grows; counteracting influences; crises. Critics (Okishio etc.) given fair space.
- **Merchant's capital (Ch 16-20):** **Tom the shopkeeper** buys Moneybags' yarn wholesale and sells
  it on; he gets a slice of the surplus as commercial profit without making anything. A gray
  one-off money-changer for money-dealing capital (Ch 19).
- **Interest and credit (Ch 21-36):** **Bea the banker** lends money-capital; profit splits into
  interest (Bea) and profit of enterprise (Moneybags). Bills of exchange, fictitious capital,
  joint-stock companies, the Bank of England and 1844 Act, crises of 1847 and 1857: verify every
  date and number against the text. Ch 36 looks back to usury before capitalism.
- **Ground-rent (Ch 37-47):** **Grandpa Ned** is the tenant capitalist farmer; a gray one-off
  **Landlord** owns the fields and takes rent. Differential rent uses Marx's soils A, B, C, D with
  his own numbers. Absolute rent, building-site rent and mining rent follow. Ned is never the landlord.
- **Revenues and classes (Ch 48-52):** the "trinity formula": capital-profit (Moneybags, Bea),
  land-rent (the Landlord), labour-wages (Sam). Ch 52 "Classes" breaks off after a page in Marx's
  manuscript; the site says so plainly. Ch 52 closes Volume III and points to the bonus Volume IV.
- Volume III was assembled by **Engels** from Marx's manuscripts and published in 1894; every
  source note says so. Some chapters (e.g. Part V) are notably fragmentary; say so where true.

## Volume IV plan (binding): Theories of Surplus-Value

The bonus volume is Marx arguing with earlier thinkers. Tiny Town frames it as **the Old Thinkers'
Shelf**: in the town library, each chapter one earlier economist "visits" (a gray one-off Figure
named after them, e.g. `name="Adam Smith"`), explains their idea with Tiny Town examples, and Marx's
reply is shown (Marx can appear as a gray one-off too). The cast tests each idea: Sam's workday,
Moneybags' workshop (c 24, v 3, s 3), Grandpa Ned's tenant farm and the Landlord, Bea's bank.

- Every page already carries a template banner saying this is not part of Capital proper and was
  first edited by Kautsky (1905-1910). Source notes name the edition used (Progress Publishers,
  three parts, on marxists.org).
- Be especially fair here: each thinker's view gets explained on its own terms first, then Marx's
  criticism, then (in "What critics say") how later economists judge both.
- Reuse earlier glossary ids in prose (physiocrats, tableau-economique, smiths-dogma,
  productive-labour, differential-rent, ...). Define only new ideas specific to the chapter.
- Ch 24 (Richard Jones) closes the bonus volume: its "Remember this" ends warmly and points to the
  wrap-up chapter, "The whole story in one animation".

## The numbers (Marx's own, Ch 7 to 9; 1 coin = 1 shilling)

- A day of Sam's labour-power is worth **3 coins** (his daily food, rent, clothes).
- **6 hours** of work create 3 coins of new value. That is the *necessary labour time*.
- Sam works a **12-hour day**, so he creates 6 coins of new value: 3 replace his wage, 3 are surplus-value.
- In 12 hours he spins 20 lb of cotton into 20 lb of yarn. Cotton 20 coins + spindle wear 4 coins = **24 coins (constant capital, c)**.
- Wage **3 coins (variable capital, v)**. Yarn sells for **30 coins**. Surplus-value **s = 3 coins**. Rate s/v = **100%**.
- Use these numbers whenever a chapter needs the workshop's figures; change them only when the
  chapter itself is about changing them (and say so on screen).
